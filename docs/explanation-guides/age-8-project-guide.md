# P.A.I.N. / Reading the pain globe

Artist-team reference | For curious eight-year-olds | English
Last updated: 2026-09-09. Version: 1.0.

Facts, comparisons, and country stories for curious eight-year-olds. Artists can explain
these ideas in their own words. Examples are optional; **artist notes** preserve source details.

## An artwork about people and the world they live in

A sore back can make walking difficult. Worry can make concentrating difficult. Changes around
a home affect people and nature. The globe connects these concerns: caring for people also
involves caring about their surroundings.

P.A.I.N. means **Personal And Interconnected with Nature**. Its longer map name is the
Personal-Planetary-Pain Map. Artist Mary Maggic leads the project with collaborators in art,
sound, design, technology, data research, and network medicine.

## Four layers, four questions

The layers work like pictures on clear sheets. Each adds information, while showing a
different part of the story.

| Layer | What it helps us explore |
| Physical | How painful body conditions can affect everyday life. |
| Emotional | What kinds of distress appear in selected writing. |
| Environmental | How temperatures change and activities produce emissions. |
| Socioeconomic | One way to compare economic resources around people's lives. |

The artwork makes **data**, recorded information, visible through shapes, colours, and words.
The marked surface suggests injury, although Earth does not feel pain as a person does.
There is no single score deciding which country suffers most.

## Data, estimates, and samples

A **measurement** records something observed, such as a temperature. An **estimate** uses
available evidence to work out a value when every detail cannot be measured directly.

A **sample** is a selection from something larger. Reading one shelf tells us about those
books; another shelf may contain different stories. The emotional layer also uses a selection.

> **Artist note:** Country symbols show available data. Physical and environmental symbols
use the strongest local value, not a national average. Brightness can indicate selection.
Small places may have words but no selectable area on the simplified map.

## Movement and sound

Rotation, zoom, and country selection bring information into focus. Emotional filters change
which themes can appear using the same texts. Moving lines help our eyes follow connections.

Composed music and interaction tones direct attention; they are not recordings of pain.
Projection mode hides the separate web survey. Participation requires no personal disclosure.

# Physical pain / Why population and place matter

Reading, playing, sleeping, and meeting friends all depend partly on how our bodies feel.
Pain can interrupt these activities even when other people cannot see it. The physical layer
makes some of that hidden difficulty visible.

## Six health categories

| Condition | What the name means |
| Headache disorders | Conditions involving headaches that can return again and again. |
| Migraine | A condition involving the nervous system, often with headache and other symptoms. |
| Low back pain | Pain around the lower back that can make movement difficult. |
| Neck pain | Pain that can affect turning the head and moving comfortably. |
| Osteoarthritis | A condition affecting joints, where bones meet, causing pain or stiffness. |
| Rheumatoid arthritis | An illness involving the body's immune system that can inflame joints. |

The immune system usually defends the body against illness; rheumatoid arthritis involves
it acting in a harmful way. Migraine belongs within the wider headache category, so these
categories do not count separate groups of people. [C3, C5-C7]

## A made-up example: more people, the same fraction

Imagine one town with **100 people** and another with **1,000 people**. In each town,
**one person in every ten** has the same painful condition.

| Imaginary town | People affected | Fraction affected |
| 100 residents | 10 people | 1 in 10 |
| 1,000 residents | 100 people | 1 in 10 |

The larger town has ten times as many affected people. It may need more care services
even though the fraction affected is unchanged.

Population information helps place the health data, contributing to strong marks near dense
parts of India. The town numbers are invented examples, not measurements from India.

> **Artist note:** IHME Global Burden of Disease and WorldPop are the source families. The
selected layer contains **15,282 condition-location observations**, **2,547 per category**;
13,183 are assigned to 155 countries. These are neither interviews nor patient counts.
The original release, reference year, health measure, upstream size, and full transformation
remain unverified. WorldPop's described family covers 2000-2020 at roughly 1 km resolution,
but a selected year is not confirmed. [S7-S8]

# Environment / Warming, heat, and emissions

**Weather** is what happens outside over a short time, such as a rainy afternoon.
**Climate** describes patterns over many years. To investigate warming, researchers compare
temperatures across periods. A warm afternoon by itself cannot tell the whole climate story.

## A made-up example: which place changed more?

Imagine two places with these average temperatures in an earlier and a later climate period.
An average combines several values to give one summary; these are not temperatures on one day.

[[DIAGRAM:warming]]

The warmer place is still warmer. The cooler place has warmed more: 14 minus 10 is 4,
while 26 minus 25 is 1. That is why a cold country can have a strong warming signal.
These numbers are invented examples, not measurements for Russia or Canada.

## What emissions mean

**Emissions** are substances released by activities, such as gases released when fuels burn.
Producing energy, making goods, and transport can produce emissions. The exhibition uses a
floating form to make this environmental pressure visible. It is not a measured cloud of smoke.

Warming asks how a place has changed; emissions concern what activities release. Country
borders locate information but do not form walls around the air. The atmosphere gives a
shared concern a shared shape.

> **Artist note:** Berkeley Earth supplies **8,100 selected temperature observations**;
2,591 are assigned to 140 countries. Climate TRACE supplies **2,339 emissions observations**;
1,873 are assigned to 146 countries. Original dataset sizes, selected releases, temperature
baseline and comparison period, and precise emissions measure remain unconfirmed. Neither
colour nor height has a verified conversion to degrees, tonnes, or personal exposure. [S9-S10]

# Economic circumstances / What an average can tell us

An economy includes making goods and providing services. Food production and building a school
are examples. **GDP** adds up the value produced within an economy. **GDP per person** divides
that total by the number of people living there. It gives one comparison of economic output.

The globe uses stronger yellow for lower GDP per person. Resources can help make schools,
transport, and care possible, but the average alone does not tell us who can use them.

## A made-up example: total and per person

Imagine two groups producing things worth units in an imaginary economy.

| Group | Total value made | Number of people | Value per person |
| Smaller group | 12 units | 3 | 12 divided by 3 = 4 |
| Larger group | 20 units | 10 | 20 divided by 10 = 2 |

The larger group makes more altogether; the smaller group makes more per person.
These answer different questions.

The smaller group's 12 units might belong mostly to one person. Dividing by three does not
make everyone receive four. GDP per person is not money handed to each resident.

## What the yellow leaves open

Resources, prices, and public services differ between people and places. Production cannot
measure kindness, happiness, dignity, or friendship. Other layers offer other questions.

> **Artist note:** World Bank **2024 GDP per capita, current US dollars**, supplies **190
values and 16 missing entries**. The display compresses the wide range using a logarithmic
scale. That does not correct for unequal sharing or differences in purchasing power. [S11]

# Emotional words / Patterns in writing

A sentence can describe loneliness and fear together. The emotional layer looks for themes
in news, posts, and complaints. A **category** groups related ideas. The AI can recognize
several categories in a passage even though the globe gives one word prominence.

## Fourteen broad categories

| Category | Meaning in this project |
| Body pain | Writing expressing physical hurt. |
| Hurt | Emotional pain without a more particular description. |
| Eco-anxiety | Worry about environmental change. |
| Uncertainty | Distress about an unstable situation or an unclear future. |
| Grief | Distress over loss, including changes to a familiar home environment. |
| Anger | Anger or outrage, sometimes connected with something felt to be wrong. |
| Hardship | Difficulty getting what is needed, or coping with insecurity and loss. |
| Displacement | Distress connected with being uprooted from a familiar home. |
| Trauma | Distress described after overwhelming experiences. |
| Loneliness | Pain around connection, relationships, and feeling alone. |
| Depression | Language describing depressive distress, beyond an ordinary brief disappointment. |
| Fear | Fear, anxiety, or panic. |
| Helplessness | Feeling unable to influence what is happening. |
| Shame | Shame, guilt, or blaming oneself. |

These categories describe writing, not national feelings or diagnoses. Two stories can
share Anger while describing different situations.

## How a country gets a word

For news, the country is where the **publisher** is based. A publisher in India can report on
an event in another country. Other sources use supplied country or location information.
The map therefore collects writing associated with a place, not interviews with all residents.

Combined text scores summarize the selected writing. The strongest of the fourteen distress
categories supplies the word: a pattern in texts, not a percentage of people.

## A language choice is also an artistic choice

Artists choose each word's language: Hindi for India, Kirundi for Burundi, Palauan for Palau.
Articles may use other languages. One choice cannot represent every language community.

> **Artist note:** The current display uses **249,438 texts**: **5,000 news articles** and
**244,438 other texts**, covering 192 countries. Where both groups exist, the news average
gets 75 percent of the weight and the other-text average 25 percent. A sole available group
gets full weight. Anger leads in 152 countries. There are 84 source-language hints and
101 displayed language codes, distinct from the classifier's 100-language coverage.

> **Artist note:** No-pain and out-of-scope scores do not compete for the displayed word.
At least one of those controls exceeds the visible distress winner in 121 of 192 countries.
A prominent word can therefore accompany weak distress evidence. Filters can change the
winning eligible category without changing the texts.

# Connections / Why the globe has lines

A **network** is a set of points joined by lines. Here, each point represents a country label.
Countries with the same displayed emotional category belong to the same group. Geography
helps decide which pairs get a direct line, so the artwork does not need every point to join
every other point.

## Delaunay: a mesh of neighbours

The exhibition currently uses a **Delaunay** network. It arranges places sharing a category
into a mesh of triangles around the globe. Triangle corners are country-label positions;
their sides supply connections. The lines follow curves around the globe's surface.

Some places connect through other points rather than a direct line. This arranges a shared
theme geographically; it does not show friendship, trade, or one country causing distress.

## Gabriel: leaving out some connections

A related mathematical idea is called a **Gabriel graph**. On a flat drawing, imagine a circle
with two matching-category points at opposite ends of its diameter. If another point from
that same category lies inside the circle, the pair does not get a direct Gabriel connection.
A point from a different category does not block it.

[[DIAGRAM:graph]]

The globe uses a corresponding three-dimensional ball. This check usually leaves fewer
lines, changing connections while keeping the same category scores and writing.

> **Artist note:** Current complete data has 192 nodes and 522 Delaunay edges across all
categories; the Gabriel comparison has 328. A selected view need not show all edges together.
For groups of fewer than four nodes, the exhibition comparison joins all pairs, so it does
not perform the full mathematical Gabriel check there. The diagram explains the mathematical
idea. Animated connections direct attention; they are not a history of spreading emotion.

# PCAI / How the AI learns to classify text

**PCAI** prepares country-associated writing and uses an AI **classifier** to identify distress
categories. It can examine far more writing than one visitor could read. People still need
to investigate what the results mean.

## Teaching examples in 100 languages

The classifier is designed to classify writing in **100 languages**. Its **synthetic dataset
is multilingual**: constructed teaching examples use many languages, rather than English
alone. Synthetic means made for this teaching purpose. A fictional sentence about missing a
friend is not testimony from a real child.

Teaching links wording and context with categories. The trained model then examines real
texts, called classification or inference. Reading more articles does not retrain it.

## Words need context

Consider these invented sentences: "I am afraid" and "I am not afraid." One small word changes
the meaning. "The character said, 'I am afraid'" describes a character's words; it does not
necessarily describe the writer. "I miss my friend and worry about tomorrow" might fit both
loneliness and uncertainty.

The model looks for patterns beyond a list of special words. Negation, quoted speech, unusual
phrasing, and several themes together can still confuse it. Languages can express similar
ideas in different ways. Being designed for 100 languages does not mean the same reliability
in each one. The complete language list appears in the artist appendix.

# PCAI / Teaching and testing are different jobs

Researchers test with separate examples, like practising maths problems and then checking
learning on different problems. Harder tests reveal difficulties that easy examples miss.

| Stage | Examples | Meaning |
| Prepared synthetic collection | 573,143 | Multilingual teaching resource covering 100 languages. |
| Used to fit the adopted model | 333,236 | Examples actually used to teach its smaller network. |
| Clean synthetic test | 15,874 | Separate evaluation examples. |
| Harder synthetic stress test | 10,429 | Challenging withheld evaluation examples. |

> **Artist note:** Adopted **version 9**, finalized **4 September 2026**, uses pretrained
**BGE-M3** to represent language numerically, followed by a project-trained network with
1,024 hidden units. Its 70 related decisions include 3 relevance, 14 broad-category,
47 fine-category, 2 safety, and 4 out-of-scope-reason decisions. These are not 70 emotions.
It reads at most 512 tokens, pieces of text that are not always whole words; later article
context can be missed. The fitted examples comprise 295,572 newer and 37,664 earlier reviewed
examples. The table's stages are not counts to add together. [S12]

> **Artist note:** Broad/fine macro-F1 is **0.9028/0.7772** on the clean synthetic test and
**0.6658/0.6552** on the stress test. F1 balances finding labelled examples with avoiding
false alarms; macro gives categories equal weight. These results are not percentages of
people understood correctly, nor verified accuracy across every real-world language.

# CCNews / From a huge library to a research collection

CCNews contains international news gathered through Common Crawl and prepared by Stanford OVAL.
The broader source is described as **about 600 million articles** in more than 100 languages.
PCAI's country-linked main collection has **about 394 million**. That is the resource often
described as roughly 400 million articles. [S1]

These numbers describe different shelves in a huge library. PCAI acquired a snapshot and
prepared country-linked texts. The upstream approximation is not an exact download count.

## What preparation changes

Duplicates can make one text seem more common. Researchers reduce repeated copies,
standardize text, check dates, and mask detected identifiers such as emails and phones.
Unclear or conflicting publisher-country assignments stay out of the country-linked collection.

This is **general news**, with no pain word required. The classifier later assesses distress.
Publisher output, language coverage, and country identification affect which voices remain.

# Country stories / Twenty-five ways to explore

These stories describe the **current exhibition**. Strongest and smallest concern the named
layer among places with available data, never all suffering. Physical and environmental
comparisons use the strongest local signal. Notes give observation counts, not patient totals.

## Story 01 / India: many people, many daily interruptions

India has a strong migraine-related signal. Where many people live close together, many
lives can be interrupted by pain. The imaginary towns explain how this can happen without
a higher fraction affected. A child studying and someone caring for a relative may need
different help with similar pain.

> **Evidence:** 266 physical observations, including 45 migraine observations. Population
helps place the health data; a high individual risk is not established.

## Story 02 / Pakistan: when a headache keeps returning

Pakistan has the strongest selected headache and migraine signals. A returning headache can
interrupt a favourite book repeatedly, helping explain a familiar condition's large effect
on daily life. Migraine belongs within the wider headache subject, so adding the two
categories as different people would count some twice.

> **Evidence:** 13 observations in each category; highest among 149 headache and 148 migraine
country comparisons. National causes are not established. [C7]

## Story 03 / Japan: movement and independence

Japan has a strong back-pain signal. Getting out of a chair and walking to meet someone help
people stay independent. Almost three in ten residents were 65 or older in 2024. Age matters
in back-pain research, although its exact contribution to this particular mark is not known.

> **Evidence:** 17 back-pain observations; strongest of 150 country signals. Population aged
65 or older: 29.3 percent in 2024. Age is contextual evidence. [C3, C8]

## Story 04 / China: one joint, different conditions

China has strong signals for two forms of arthritis. Joints include knees and fingers.
Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis affect them through different processes, but both
can make holding a cup or climbing stairs harder. The shared word suggests a connection;
the different names help distinguish the conditions.

> **Evidence:** 135 osteoarthritis and 137 rheumatoid-arthritis observations. Each has the
strongest signal in its selected country comparison, with 149 and 153 countries. [C5-C6]

## Story 05 / Egypt: turning towards someone

Egypt has a strong neck-pain signal. Turning towards someone who called our name seems
simple when it feels comfortable. Pain can make that turn difficult, affecting reading or
travel too. The example connects a distant mark with familiar movement without identifying
a job or habit that caused Egypt's result.

> **Evidence:** 12 neck-pain observations; strongest among 148 country signals. No specific
local occupational or lifestyle cause has been established.

## Story 06 / United Kingdom: what appearances leave out

The United Kingdom has a strong rheumatoid-arthritis signal. Someone may look well while
their joints feel painful or swollen. Opening a jar or fastening clothing can take extra
effort. What we see from outside does not always match what a body experiences, which helps
explain why support matters.

> **Evidence:** 9 rheumatoid-arthritis observations; second-highest among 153 country signals.
The condition involves immune-related inflammation, not a diagnosis made by the map. [C6]

## Story 07 / Guyana and Vanuatu: quieter does not mean complete

Guyana and Vanuatu have low physical signals. This layer covers six conditions, not every
kind of pain. A school chart recording reading books cannot tell us about music or sport.
Similarly, a quiet physical mark answers one question while leaving experiences outside
the selection.

> **Evidence:** 12 physical observations per country, two per condition. Their overall local
maxima are second- and third-lowest among 155 observed countries.

## Story 08 / Russia: cold and changing

Russia has the strongest local warming signal here. Cold places can warm substantially,
just as the imaginary cooler place warmed more. Arctic research describes interacting
processes, including snow and ice changes. Russia contains many climates, so this regional
context does not explain every place within its huge area.

> **Evidence:** 363 temperature observations; highest local signal among 140 countries.
Arctic research supplies regional context, not the selected observation baseline. [C1]

## Story 09 / Canada: beyond today's coat

Canada also has a strong warming signal. Wearing a coat tells us about today's conditions;
the warming layer concerns change across years. A familiar landscape can have a changing
climate. This connects an everyday decision, what to wear, with a longer question about
surroundings that people, plants, and animals depend on.

> **Evidence:** 220 temperature observations; second-highest local signal among 140 countries.
The result is not a uniform temperature increase across Canada. [C1]

## Story 10 / Uruguay: a zero with a precise meaning

Uruguay's temperature signal is zero in this picture, with observations present. That differs
from an empty entry. It does not mean Uruguay has never warmed. We still need to confirm
which earlier period the source comparison uses. The zero answers a particular question;
it cannot tell us about every environmental change.

> **Evidence:** Two temperature observations; zero local maximum, tied at the observed minimum.
It is a transformed display value, not zero degrees of climate change.

## Story 11 / Iceland: a place is more than its name

Iceland has a very small temperature signal here. Its name brings ice to mind, but a name
is not a measurement. Weather, landscape, warming, and how easily a place could be harmed describe
different things. A faint mark invites investigation of this comparison rather than a
decision about everything changing in Iceland.

> **Evidence:** Three temperature observations; smallest positive local maximum among 140
observed countries. The comparison does not summarize Iceland's entire climate.

## Story 12 / Russia: a different environmental question

Russia also has a strong emissions signal. Emissions concern substances released by
activities; warming concerns temperature change. Both layers can describe the same country
without measuring the same thing. The floating atmosphere connects local activities with
shared surroundings: air does not stop at a line on a political map.

> **Evidence:** 273 emissions observations; strongest local signal among 146 countries.
It is neither a national emissions total nor a measure of what someone breathes. [S10]

## Story 13 / Iran and Iraq: activity made visible

Iran and Iraq have strong emissions signals. Making electricity, transporting goods, and
producing materials can release substances. The artwork makes that pressure noticeable.
It does not establish which activity explains these marks; that needs specific evidence
about the selected observations.

> **Evidence:** Iran has 22 emissions observations and Iraq five; their local maxima are
third- and fifth-highest among 146 countries. No sector is identified by the visual. [S10]

## Story 14 / Guinea-Bissau: low emissions, another open question

Guinea-Bissau is near the low end of the emissions view. What a place releases differs
from the environmental changes it experiences. A low mark cannot establish that people or ecosystems face little environmental danger. One observation also leaves many local details outside this
particular picture.

> **Evidence:** One emissions observation, near the low end of 146 country signals. There
is no assigned temperature observation or selected emotional observation for this country.

## Story 15 / Burundi: resources around a life

Burundi has the strongest yellow because its GDP per person is lowest here. Resources can
help provide schools, transport, and care. World Bank context describes development
constraints, including low-productivity agriculture. This explains some economic background,
while the colour leaves each family's resources and access to help unknown.

> **Evidence:** One 2024 GDP value, about US$216 per person; lowest of 190 available values.
Country context is not a calculation of each cause's contribution. [C2, S11]

## Story 16 / Luxembourg: who contributes to the total?

Luxembourg has high GDP per person and weak yellow. Cross-border workers contribute to
its production while living outside the resident population used in the division. An
average therefore needs both parts understood: what goes into the total, and which people
are counted underneath it.

> **Evidence:** One 2024 GDP value, about US$137,782 per person; third-highest of 190 values.
Cross-border worker accounting is verified context, not a typical resident's salary. [C4]

## Story 17 / Monaco: resources and feelings

Monaco has the highest GDP per person here and the weakest yellow. It also displays Hurt.
Production and emotional language answer different questions. A fictional person can have
resources and still miss a friend. This explains how the layers differ without claiming
what Monaco's residents actually feel.

> **Evidence:** GDP about US$288,002 per person, highest of 190 values. Hurt comes from
27 news articles and 1,034 other texts; these are separate kinds of evidence.

## Story 18 / India: a large total and a large population

India separates total economic size from output per person. The imaginary larger group
made more altogether but less per person. A large country can likewise have a large total
and many residents. Yellow uses the per-person comparison, leaving another question open:
how are resources shared within the country?

> **Evidence:** One 2024 GDP value, about US$2,592 per person, forty-sixth-lowest of 190 values.
This is not total GDP or the percentage of people experiencing poverty. [S11]

## Story 19 / Yemen: a missing piece

Yemen has no available GDP value here, although Grief is present in its emotional layer.
Each source brings different information. A missing entry means this evidence cannot
answer that question, like a question left unanswered on a worksheet rather than an
answer of zero.

> **Evidence:** No selected 2024 GDP observation; the emotional observation remains present.
Missing economic data does not mean no economic hardship.

## Story 20 / Burundi: anger at something unfair

Burundi's texts have a strong Anger result, a category including outrage at perceived
wrongdoing. A fictional person might be angry because a friend was treated unfairly.
This helps explain the category without calling a population aggressive. The actual
texts' reasons require evidence beyond their label.

> **Evidence:** 28 news articles plus two other texts; highest selected anger score among
192 countries. The exhibition weights the two source-group averages 75/25.

## Story 21 / Palau: one article is a small window

Palau's Uncertainty word comes from one article. One page can tell an interesting story
without explaining a country's life. The AI gave a higher score to "out-of-scope," meaning
writing that does not fit the question being asked. The map still chooses among distress
words, so a visible word need not mean strong evidence of distress.

> **Evidence:** One publisher-linked news article, no other texts. Uncertainty score 0.1557;
out-of-scope score 0.6492. Neither value is a percentage of residents.

## Story 22 / Timor-Leste: one category, several possible stories

Timor-Leste has a strong Anger theme in eight texts. An unfair rule, a disagreement, or
a loss could produce angry language in fictional stories. The category does not tell us
which explanation fits the actual writing. Eight texts give a starting point while leaving
many experiences unrepresented.

> **Evidence:** Five news articles plus three other texts; second-highest selected anger
score among 192 countries. National causes are not established.

## Story 23 / Sao Tome and Principe: concern about a changing home

Sao Tome and Principe shows Eco-anxiety. A fictional character might worry about changes
to a familiar environment. Such language expresses concern; a temperature record supplies
a different kind of evidence. Together the layers distinguish changes around a place
from how concerns appear in writing.

> **Evidence:** 27 publisher-linked news articles, no other-text contribution; highest
eco-anxiety score among 192 countries. It is not a population anxiety rate.

## Story 24 / Oman: pain in words and health data

Oman's emotional word is Body pain, found in writing. Its physical surface comes from
health estimates. A sentence about a character's sore back and a study of back-pain burden
can both matter while answering different questions. Their shared subject does not make
the text score a patient count.

> **Evidence:** 27 news articles plus 968 other texts; highest body-pain language score among
192 countries. The separate physical layer has 36 mapped observations.

## Story 25 / Comoros: visible through one source

Comoros has economic information but no selected emotional texts. Imagine a library with
a geography book about a place but no storybook from there. The missing book limits the
collection; it does not mean the place has no stories. Sources determine what the globe
can make visible.

> **Evidence:** GDP about US$1,858 per person in 2024; zero news and zero other-text records in
the emotional selection. Missing emotional evidence is not evidence of no distress.

# Useful words and optional connections

Maps, stories, maths, and art offer different ways into the same facts. Comparisons are
optional, with no required route.

Possible connections include transparent sheets for layers, library shelves for samples,
and fictional characters for overlapping feelings. The worked examples explain numerical
comparisons. Each leaves room for a visitor's own questions.

## A working glossary

| Word | Meaning |
| Average | One summary made by adding values and dividing by how many there are. |
| Category | A group of related things or ideas. |
| Classifier | A model that identifies categories in material such as writing. |
| Climate | Weather patterns studied over many years. |
| Data | Recorded information used to investigate a question. |
| Emissions | Substances released by activities. |
| Estimate | A value worked out from available evidence, with limits. |
| GDP per person | Economic output divided by resident population. |
| Network | Points joined by connections. |
| Pattern | A relationship or feature found across examples. |
| Sample | A selection from a larger collection. |

**Missing differs from zero.** Zero is an observed answer in a comparison; missing means
unavailable. Understanding the question and source helps us read the picture.
