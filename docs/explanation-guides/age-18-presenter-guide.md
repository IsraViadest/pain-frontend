# P.A.I.N. / A guide to looking closely

Audience: age 18 and high-school graduates. Presenter edition.
Last updated: 2026-09-09. Version: 1.0.

Nine spoken routes through the exhibition, with source notes and 25 country stories.

## Before you begin

P.A.I.N. means Personal And Interconnected with Nature. The Personal-Planetary-Pain Map brings
human and environmental indicators into one artwork. It invites attention and interpretation;
its four layers do not form a validated universal measure of suffering.

Choose one voice and one duration. M is museum guide, S is science educator, and A is artist
talk. Each script stands alone. Directions in shaded boxes are for you, not spoken words.
Timings assume 120-140 spoken words per minute plus the explicitly timed pauses. Rehearse once:
room acoustics, questions, and globe operation change delivery time. The longer scripts include
complete explanations and observation periods; no separate story has to be inserted to finish them.

The scripts use a few rounded numbers. The background notes retain denominators, definitions,
and references. Source IDs such as [L1] and [S1] are explained in the reference section.
Public-source context is separated from what has actually been checked in the exhibition data.

## The three routes

| Route | Use it when | Where to begin |
| Museum guide | Visitors want an engaging tour of what they see. | M1, M2, or M3 |
| Science educator | Visitors want to understand how evidence becomes an image. | S1, S2, or S3 |
| Artist talk | Visitors want to explore the work's questions and metaphors. | A1, A2, or A3 |

All three routes explain the same selected exhibition: combined-v2 emotional data, 2024 GDP per
capita, six physical conditions, temperature and emissions fields, and a Delaunay category
network. Changing the voice changes emphasis, not the facts. Gabriel graphs are explained as
the related alternative implemented during development. [L1-L5]

## What every presenter should know

- The emotional input contains 249,438 analysed records from six sources and covers 192 countries.
- A displayed emotion is a model-selected category in sampled texts, not the identity of a people.
- The country profiles use the highest mapped physical, temperature, or emissions value inside
  each country polygon. Those values are not national averages.
- The socioeconomic layer is an artistic proxy based on GDP per person, not a poverty survey.
- Network lines express geographic construction within matching categories, not measured influence.
- Missing observations and a low or zero score mean different things.
- Original health and environmental processing details remain partly unverified. The notes say
  exactly which details. Do not turn those gaps into confident causal stories.

# M1 / Museum guide / 2-3 minutes

> Start with all layers visible. Point to the globe, then the words, surface, atmosphere, and yellow areas.

Welcome to P.A.I.N. The name means Personal And Interconnected with Nature. This globe asks us
to look at human suffering and the condition of our environment together. It combines art,
data, and a way of drawing connections.

There are four layers. The words above countries belong to the emotional layer. A computer
model has examined a selected collection of news, social posts, and complaints. It scores
different kinds of distress, and the artwork displays the strongest retained category for each
country. The word is a clue about those texts, not a description of everyone living there.

The physical layer draws on estimates for six painful conditions, including migraine and back
pain. Population data helps place country-based information across the map. The surface turns
that information into dents and dots. A dot does not stand for one patient.

The environmental layer brings together temperature change and emissions. Its haze is a visual
metaphor: it is not a photograph of smoke. The yellow socioeconomic layer uses economic output
per person. Lower values produce a stronger yellow signal, but money alone cannot describe a
person's life or pain.

When we select an emotional category, lines connect countries sharing that category. The current
network uses a geographic rule called Delaunay. The lines do not mean that one country caused
another country's distress.

India is a useful place to look closely. Its physical signal is high, but the country symbol
shows a peak among mapped points, not the average risk of an individual Indian. Population
concentration and the way the data is placed both matter.

As you explore, ask three things: what am I seeing, what information produced it, and what is
still missing? The artwork gives us a shared place to begin that conversation.

> Invite one observation. Sources and numerical background: [L1-L5], [S1-S11].

# M2 / Museum guide / 5-10 minutes

> Begin on all layers. Pause briefly between layers so visitors can locate each visual cue.

Before we explain this globe, take a moment to notice what caught your eye. Perhaps it was a
word, a bright country, a dent in the surface, or the haze around it. That first attraction is
part of the work. P.A.I.N. brings several kinds of information into one image so that we can
move between something visible and questions that are much harder to see.

The name means Personal And Interconnected with Nature. The artwork calls this globe the
Personal-Planetary-Pain Map. It places bodily conditions, emotional language, environmental
change, and economic circumstances together. Their coexistence is the starting point of the
artistic conversation. It does not mean that all four have been measured with one common
scientific instrument.

Let's begin with the physical surface. Its source attribution is the Global Burden of Disease
study at IHME, combined with WorldPop population information. The exhibition contains six
conditions: headache disorders, low back pain, migraine, neck pain, osteoarthritis, and
rheumatoid arthritis. Each has 2,547 spatial records, making 15,282 records altogether.

Those records are not individual people. The project describes placing country-based health
information using population data. Places where people are concentrated can therefore carry
concentrated mapped values. The rendering then turns points into a continuous-looking surface,
with overlapping dents and a dotted texture. The many visible dots are drawing elements, not
a census of patients.

India gives us a practical example. Its physical country profile reaches about 0.943 on the
map's normalized scale, with the peak coming from a migraine-labelled point. The country
profile selects the highest point inside the boundary. It does not average all the people,
all the cities, or all the conditions. That helps explain why the map can look intense around
a densely populated place without establishing that every resident has unusually high risk.

Now look above the surface. The environmental layer has two separate inputs: temperature
change attributed to Berkeley Earth, and emission-related values attributed to Climate TRACE.
There are 8,100 temperature points and 2,339 emissions points in the exported map data.
The red temperature treatment represents change, not simply how hot a place feels today.
A cold region can experience substantial warming. Russia and Canada have high country-profile
warming peaks in this dataset, which makes that distinction especially useful.

The emissions atmosphere is also an interpretation. It lets us see an otherwise abstract
quantity, but the cloud is not measured smoke or local exposure. We still need the original
processing details to identify the exact gas measure and period behind these exported values.
We can explain what the source and the display are doing while being honest about that gap.

The yellow layer is more directly traceable. It uses World Bank GDP per person for 2024,
expressed in current US dollars. GDP is economic production. Dividing by population gives a
per-person comparison; it does not tell us what the typical person earns. The artwork compresses
the enormous range using a logarithm, then reverses it so lower GDP per person gives stronger
yellow. Burundi and Luxembourg make a useful contrast, but neither number contains a complete
account of living conditions, inequality, or care.

Finally, consider the words. The selected emotional input contains just under a quarter of a
million analysed records from six sources. News forms one group; five collections of social
and complaint texts form another. The country score gives the news mean three quarters of the
weight and the expression mean one quarter when both exist. Those are chosen weights, not
the proportions of articles in the collection.

A custom classifier scores 14 distress categories. The highest retained category supplies the
word above the country, translated through a country-language lexicon. Many categories can have
some signal at once. A single visible word simplifies that much richer set of values.
For news, the country usually identifies the publisher's origin, not where a reported event
happened. We are looking at classified texts connected to countries, not a national opinion poll.

> Select an emotional category. Allow 20 seconds to follow the network.

The connecting lines belong to a Delaunay network built from geographic positions within that
category. Gabriel is a related, more selective rule that the project also explored. In both
cases the connection is constructed from position and category membership. Its animation
guides our attention; it does not show distress spreading through time.

The most useful habit is to hold two things together: curiosity about the image and care about
what its data means. A blank country can mean missing information. A small sample can make an
extreme score unstable. A country average, a local peak, and an individual experience are
different things. With those distinctions in mind, the globe becomes a place to ask better
questions about one another and the environments we share.

> Optional discussion: invite a visitor to compare two layers over the same country. [L1-L5], [S1-S11]

# M3 / Museum guide / 15-20 minutes

> Allow the specified 210 seconds of looking and conversation. Keep those pauses even if the room is quiet.

Welcome. Before we put names and numbers to this work, let us give ourselves time to look.
This is P.A.I.N., Personal And Interconnected with Nature. It asks how our personal experiences
of suffering sit within the larger world that supports us. The globe is called the
Personal-Planetary-Pain Map. It combines art, machine learning, geographic data, and a network
that grows in response to our selection.

You may already recognize that this is an unusual map. Familiar countries sit on a surface
that looks wounded. Words appear in different languages. A coloured atmosphere seems to cling
to parts of the planet. The work gives those forms a shared space, while the information behind
them comes from very different sources.

> Looking pause: 30 seconds. Invite people to choose one feature they want explained.

We will follow four questions. What does each layer show? Where does its information come from?
What choices turn information into an image? And what can a visitor reasonably conclude?
These questions let us enjoy the artwork without assuming that its appearance is a direct
measurement of everybody's suffering.

Start with the physical layer. The project attributes its health data to the Institute for
Health Metrics and Evaluation's Global Burden of Disease study. That study produces health
estimates from many kinds of evidence. Here we have six condition categories: headache
disorders, migraine, low back pain, neck pain, osteoarthritis, and rheumatoid arthritis.
Some names are familiar because we have experienced them ourselves; others may describe the
experience of someone we know.

The map contains 15,282 spatial records for these conditions. There are 2,547 per condition.
This tells us the size of the exported map input, not how many patients were consulted and not
how many people have a condition. Those counts belong to different stages of the evidence.
The source attribution says that country-based health information is distributed using
WorldPop population data. This is a way to estimate where a national burden might be situated,
rather than a separate health survey conducted at every dot.

The surface adds another transformation. Points create overlapping depressions; smoothing and
rendering make them look like a continuous material. Dots help reveal that surface. They are
visual samples generated by the artwork. A deeper-looking place can reflect both the input
values and how nearby points accumulate. You cannot count the dots to count patients.

Let's use India to make this concrete. Its physical profile takes its highest mapped value
from a migraine-labelled point, about 0.943 on a scale from zero to one. India has 266 contributing
physical records inside the polygons used for this calculation. The country symbol shows the
largest of those values, not their average. If we used an average or a population-adjusted
rate, we would be asking a different question and might get a different ordering.

The population-based placement gives us a useful explanation for why crowded areas may appear
concentrated. It does not, by itself, identify a medical cause. To explain an unusual migraine
rate, for example, we would need to know the original health measure, its year, age structure,
and uncertainty. Those details have not all been recovered for this export. We should keep
the visible pattern and the medical explanation as separate steps.

Other countries make the distinction richer. Pakistan reaches a normalized peak of one in
headache disorders and migraine. Japan reaches one in low back pain. China reaches one in
osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. The condition behind a maximum matters. These values
do not establish that the countries tie for an identical overall amount of suffering, because
the original normalization across conditions has not been fully reconstructed.

> Looking pause: 40 seconds. Compare a strong physical region with a weaker one. Ask what extra information would make the comparison fair.

Move now from the surface to the atmosphere. The environmental layer has temperature-change
and emissions inputs. The temperature source is attributed to Berkeley Earth. Its exported
map contains 8,100 points. The emissions source is attributed to Climate TRACE, with 2,339
points. These numbers tell us the size of this map's input, not the size of either institution's
complete database.

Temperature change is a surprisingly easy idea to misread. A place can remain cold while
warming substantially compared with its earlier climate. A place that feels very hot today
does not necessarily have the highest long-term change in this particular dataset. Russia and
Canada have high country-profile warming peaks here. Research assessed by the IPCC describes
amplified Arctic warming, with several interacting processes, including changes in snow and
ice reflectivity. That is relevant regional context, not a reconstruction of every point on
this map.

The country profile again uses a maximum. Russia is large and contains many contributing
locations. More observed locations create more opportunities for an extreme value. The
comparison therefore involves both a climate signal and a summary rule. A high local peak
cannot simply be renamed a country's average warming.

The emissions atmosphere is equally important to read carefully. Climate TRACE publishes
emissions estimates for different gases, sectors, and periods. The local category is called
CO2, but its exact original measure and time window still require confirmation. The visual
haze is an artistic device. It is not a sensor reading of the air someone breathes, and its
height does not represent a measured altitude. A dramatic cloud can start a conversation about
emissions, but it cannot by itself tell us the exposure of nearby residents.

> Looking pause: 30 seconds. Ask visitors to distinguish a change in temperature from an absolute temperature, using any familiar place.

The socioeconomic layer uses a quantity for which we can trace the displayed conversion:
World Bank GDP per capita in 2024. GDP describes economic output. Per capita means divided by
population. The units here are current US dollars per person. They are not purchasing-power
adjusted and they are not the income of the median household.

The available values stretch from about 216 dollars per person for Burundi to about 288,002
for Monaco. A simple linear scale over that range would compress many countries near one
end. The project uses a logarithm to make proportional differences easier to display, then
inverts the result: lower GDP per person gives stronger yellow. Burundi sets the high endpoint
of that artistic signal. Monaco sets the low endpoint. A zero at that endpoint does not mean
that nobody in Monaco suffers.

Burundi and Luxembourg give us a useful story beyond the endpoints. The World Bank describes
structural constraints on Burundi's economy, including low-productivity agriculture and
landlocked geography. Luxembourg's national statistics office explains that cross-border
workers contribute to production while the per-person denominator counts residents. These
are reasons to understand the economic statistic before treating it as a description of
individual experience. The artwork calls the signal socioeconomic pain, but the source
variable itself remains economic output per person.

Now return to the emotional words. This layer is built through PCAI, which prepares and
country-codes text, and pcai-classifier, which scores it. The selected exhibition uses 249,438
analysed records. Five thousand are from a news probe. The other 244,438 come from five
expression collections: country-context tweets, civic-participation tweets, coordinate-linked
texts, consumer complaints, and geotagged microblogs.

The dates and countries are unevenly covered. Most of the selected news records have recorded
years 2015 or 2016. The full input is not a simultaneous global snapshot. News geography usually
means the publisher's origin. A publisher in one country may report on events across the world.
Other source collections assign geography in other ways, such as profile locations or supplied
country metadata. None of that is interchangeable with interviewing a representative group
of residents about their current feelings.

The classifier learned from synthetic training examples. It then scores these real input texts
for categories of distress. Different categories can overlap. A text can express both grief
and anger. The country score averages those model outputs, with a chosen three-to-one weight
between news and expressions where both are available. The artwork then selects the strongest
of the 14 retained distress categories and displays a word through its language lexicon.

Here is why sample size belongs in a story. Palau has only one selected news record and no
expression records. Burundi has 28 news records and two expressions. An extreme category score
in either case demands attention to those small samples before a national explanation.
The word above the country summarizes the chosen material; it does not speak for everybody.

> Select anger and allow 40 seconds to follow the network from the highlighted origin.

The lines join countries assigned the same category. They use geographic label locations,
not the locations of individual authors. The current rule is Delaunay: on the sphere the code
constructs a network from the edges of a three-dimensional convex hull. Imagine a tight shell
stretched around the category's points: the edges of that shell supply the Delaunay connections.
The Gabriel rule,
implemented during development, starts with those candidate edges and keeps a connection only
when its diameter ball contains no other category point inside. We will use a small diagram
later if you want to see that test.

The significant point for a visitor is simpler: this is a geometric construction. It does not
measure communication, migration, friendship, shared policy, or the spread of emotion.
The animation has a direction because someone selected a starting point. That direction is
a way of revealing the image, not evidence of a historical sequence.

Try changing an emotional category filter. Removing a category does more than hide a word:
the remaining scores can produce a different winner, so a country's network membership can
change. The stored text scores have not changed. We have changed the question used to select
what is visible.

> Reflection pause: 70 seconds. Invite one observation, one possible explanation, and one thing the map alone cannot establish.

Take those three observations with you as you continue exploring the projection.
That distinction is a good place to finish. The artwork makes distant conditions available
to shared attention. The data gives us reasons to look, compare, and ask. Our responsibility
as viewers is to notice the person who disappears inside an average, the place missing from
a dataset, and the assumptions hidden inside a beautiful image. P.A.I.N. does not complete
the conversation about care. It gives us a place to begin it together.

> References: [L1-L5], [S1-S11], [C1-C4]. No country-causal claim is established by a map rank alone.

# S1 / Science educator / 2-3 minutes

> Point to one visible feature for each step: source, transformation, display.

Think of this globe as the end of several data pipelines. A pipeline begins with observations
or estimates, applies a series of choices, and ends with a visual mark. To understand the mark,
we need to follow that journey backwards.

The emotional layer begins with 249,438 selected text records from six sources. A trained model
assigns scores to categories such as anger, grief, fear, and hardship. Country-level means are
combined, then the highest retained category supplies the displayed word. The result describes
model outputs on that collection. It is not the percentage of residents feeling that emotion.

The physical layer contains 15,282 map records for six health conditions. The project describes
distributing country-based estimates using population information. The country profile selects
the highest mapped value inside the boundary. A maximum is sensitive to where points are placed
and how many opportunities there are to encounter an extreme.

Temperature change and emissions are separate environmental inputs. Their atmospheric appearance
is a rendering choice, not a measurement of smoke thickness. The socioeconomic input is GDP
per person in 2024. A reversed logarithmic transformation gives lower GDP values a stronger
yellow signal. That choice does not turn economic output into a clinical pain scale.

The network adds another rule. Countries sharing the selected emotional category become nodes
at geographic label positions. Delaunay geometry supplies the current edges. The related Gabriel
rule keeps fewer candidate edges using an empty-diameter-ball test. Neither rule measures
whether two countries influence one another.

There is a useful scientific question at every step: what is the unit, what is the denominator,
and which choices could change the result? That is how we can appreciate this artwork while
keeping its evidence and its interpretation clear.

> Optional final question: what extra information would turn one of these comparisons into a testable claim? [L1-L5]

# S2 / Science educator / 5-10 minutes

> Keep the source-to-display diagram available. This script includes one 30-second reasoning pause.

We can read P.A.I.N. as a sequence of scientific and artistic decisions. Start with a source.
Choose some of its records. Assign them to places. Calculate a summary. Map that summary to a
visual feature. Every stage can change what the final image appears to say.

The emotional layer is a particularly clear example. PCAI prepares and country-codes source
texts; pcai-classifier scores them. The selected exhibition combines 5,000 news records with
244,438 records from five social and complaint collections. This is a selected analytical
product containing 249,438 records, not the entire upstream collection and not a count of
unique people.

Geography requires a definition. For the news component, the country describes the publisher's
organizational origin. It does not necessarily describe the article's subject. In the other
collections, geography may come from a supplied country field, a location in profile metadata,
or coordinate context. A country label therefore does not automatically mean the same thing
across all six sources.

The classifier converts text into numerical representations and uses a trained prediction head
to score distress categories. Training examples and analysed texts are separate: synthetic
examples teach the model, while the selected real texts produce the exhibition's scores.
The predictions remain diagnostic. They have not been validated as measurements of national
emotional prevalence.

How do thousands of scores turn into one country value? First calculate a mean for news and
a mean for the pooled expressions in that country. When both exist, multiply the news mean
by 0.75 and the expression mean by 0.25, then add them. For an illustrative category with a
news mean of 0.4 and an expression mean of 0.2, the result is 0.35. Those example numbers are
invented to demonstrate the arithmetic. The weighting is a design choice, independent of how
many rows each group contains.

The display picks the highest of 14 distress categories. Other outputs, including no-pain and
out-of-scope scores, are excluded from this winner selection. That means a country receives
a distress word even when an excluded control score is higher. A visible word cannot establish
that most of the country's texts, much less most residents, express distress.

> Reasoning pause: 30 seconds. If a country has just one sampled article, what would you want to know before explaining its score?

Now compare that pipeline with the physical layer. Its six condition surfaces use health
estimates attributed to IHME and population-based placement attributed to WorldPop. We can
inspect 2,547 map records for each condition. The original health measure and full allocation
formula have not yet been recovered, so the exported intensity cannot responsibly be labelled
as a prevalence percentage or a number of patients.

For a country profile, the code selects the maximum point inside its polygon, across the
physical conditions. India has a peak near 0.943 from a migraine-labelled point. That is a
statement about the current exported surface and the maximum rule. A national mean, a
population rate, or an age-standardized comparison would be a different statistic.

Temperature and emissions also use point maxima for the country symbols. Temperature change
is different from absolute temperature: a cold region can warm strongly. Emissions are different
from the concentration of a gas in the air. The atmosphere around this globe is a way of
drawing those inputs, not a measurement of what someone would inhale at that location.

The socioeconomic transformation is fully visible in the application. The input is World Bank
GDP per person in current US dollars for 2024. The app takes its logarithm, rescales it between
the available minimum and maximum, and reverses the result. Lower output per person therefore
produces a larger displayed signal. A logarithm compresses ratios across a very broad range;
it does not adjust for inequality or purchasing power.

Finally, the network uses countries sharing the displayed category. Delaunay edges are built
from geographic positions on a sphere. Gabriel is a related option that tests whether another
point lies inside the ball whose diameter is a candidate connection. The displayed animation
reveals the network from a selected origin. No evidence of causal transmission enters that
construction.

The scientific skill this artwork invites is asking what would change under another reasonable
choice. Change a sample, a country rule, a mean into a maximum, or the set of allowed categories,
and the image may change. Identifying those choices does not remove the artwork's meaning.
It helps us locate that meaning accurately and ask which next observations would strengthen it.

> Sources: [L1-L5], [S1-S11]. The arithmetic example is illustrative, not an observed country.

# S3 / Science educator / 15-20 minutes

> Use the diagrams and the complete 210 seconds of discussion pauses. This is an explanation of the data pipeline, not a clinical interpretation.

Today we will investigate how this image is made. The useful starting point is not whether a
country looks bright or dark. It is the chain of decisions between a source record and the
mark we can see. P.A.I.N. combines several such chains, and understanding their differences
is essential to interpreting the result.

Our working model has five stages: source, selection, geographic assignment, numerical summary,
and visual encoding. We will follow the emotional layer through those stages, compare the
other layers, and then examine the network. At the end, we should be able to name both a
supported observation and a claim that would need more evidence.

The emotional source product contains 249,438 analysed rows from six collections. Five thousand
rows come from a Stanford OVAL CCNews probe. The other 244,438 come from country-context tweets,
DEMOTEC civic-participation tweets, Yachay coordinate-linked texts, CFPB complaint narratives,
and CMU GeoText microblogs. Their sizes are highly unequal. The country-context tweet collection
alone contributes 166,837 rows. CMU contributes 814. These are records, not unique participants.

The combined input has observations for 192 countries and 84 distinct language hints. A language
hint is metadata, not proof of accurate classification in that language. The source years are
also uneven. In the selected news probe, 4,692 of 5,000 records have recorded years 2015 or
2016. So a label on the globe must not be narrated as today's real-time national mood.
Even the meaning of a date deserves care: a recorded year can depend on upstream metadata,
and later source items need their publication-versus-crawl semantics checked.

Country assignment is the next decision. In the news component, country is based on publisher
origin. Domain reference information and carefully handled country-code domains support that
assignment. A news outlet in one country can publish a report about another. Consequently,
the map's news country is not automatically the location of the suffering described.

Expressions use a range of existing geographic context. A supplied country field, a profile
location, a coordinate bounding box, and a complaint location are different forms of evidence.
They should not all be called an author's precise location. The pipeline selects eligible
records under its geographic rules; the meaning of those rules must travel with the resulting
country statistics.

> Discussion pause: 40 seconds. Imagine a newspaper based in one country reporting on a disaster elsewhere. Which location would answer which question?

Now we reach the classifier. The system uses BGE-M3 text representations and a trained
prediction head. It learns from synthetic examples, then produces saved predictions on the
real selected input. Training and inference are different activities. A large training package
does not mean that the same number of real people contributed to the exhibition.

The model first considers whether a text contains in-scope distress, then scores distress
groups. In the public score composition, a group score combines the scope score with the
conditional score for that group. For example, hypothetical scores of 0.8 and 0.5 yield 0.4
when multiplied. This is model arithmetic, not evidence that 40 percent of a population has
a condition. The distinction matters because probability-like outputs can look more definitive
than the validation actually supports.

The 14 groups can overlap. Anger, grief, and hardship are not mutually exclusive experiences.
Their scores need not add to one. The model also has controls such as no-pain and out-of-scope,
plus finer outputs not shown as the primary country word. Its saved acceptance status remains
diagnostic: a completed computation is not the same as a validated national measurement.

The country aggregation has another important detail. It averages group scores across all
selected rows in each cohort. It is not counting only rows above a classification threshold.
For a country with both sources, the result is three quarters of the news mean plus one
quarter of the pooled expression mean. Countries with only one cohort use that available
cohort. This means that a small news sample can receive greater influence than a much larger
expression sample. Weight represents a chosen importance, not sample proportion.

Let us work through invented numbers. Suppose a category's news scores average 0.4 and its
expression scores average 0.2. The weighted country value is 0.75 times 0.4 plus 0.25 times
0.2: 0.3 plus 0.05, or 0.35. If we instead pooled all records equally, the result would depend
on how many records each cohort contained. Those two methods answer differently weighted
questions, even with exactly the same input texts.

> Calculation pause: 40 seconds. Ask whether adding a thousand expression rows necessarily changes the chosen 75/25 weights. It does not.

There is a further distinction between numerical precision and evidential confidence. A saved
score can have four decimal places because a computer calculated and rounded it that way.
Those decimal places do not tell us how close it is to a human judgement. To assess that, we
would need suitable independently labelled examples and a clear definition of the intended
measurement. We would also need to consider whether the evaluation covers the languages,
sources, and kinds of text used here. Repeating the same computation can establish that it
is reproducible. It cannot by itself establish that the result represents national experience.
This is why the guide retains the diagnostic status alongside its exact counts and formulas.

The displayed word adds a final selection. It takes the highest score among the 14 distress
categories. It excludes the no-pain and out-of-scope controls from the competition. Palau shows
why that matters. It has one selected news row, its displayed winner is uncertainty, and its
out-of-scope score is higher than all the distress categories. The artistic word therefore
cannot be treated as a finding that the country is predominantly distressed.

There is another useful intervention you can perform in the interface. Exclude a category.
The stored scores stay the same, but the highest remaining category may change. The country's
word and network membership can change with it. This is an excellent demonstration that a
display depends on a question posed to the data, as well as on the data itself.

The physical layer follows a different chain. Its source attribution is IHME's Global Burden
of Disease study, with WorldPop population information used to distribute country-based
estimates. The six conditions contribute 15,282 spatial rows. The exact health release,
measure, age and sex strata, population year, and normalization formula have not all been
recovered. That prevents us from translating a normalized point directly into a prevalence
rate, case count, or disability measure.

What we can verify is the country profile calculation: assign points to the displayed country
polygons and keep the highest value. India has 266 physical records contributing to that
calculation and a maximum of about 0.943. A point's contribution is not a patient count.
If two countries have different numbers of mapped points, the country with more points has
more opportunities to contain an extreme. This does not prove why either maximum is high,
but it identifies a concrete comparison issue.

> Comparison pause: 40 seconds. Contrast the largest exam mark in a class with its average mark. Then return to the country maximum.

The environmental layer has 8,100 temperature points and 2,339 emissions points. Temperature

per person look strong in the artistic pain layer. Neither operation adds information about
income distribution, household costs, social support, or subjective suffering. Combining this

Let us finish the construction with the graph. Each node is a country label location converted

group. Within each group, the Delaunay construction uses the edges of a three-dimensional
convex hull. The result is rendered as curved great-circle arcs lifted above the globe.
Imagine a tight shell stretched around the category's points. Its edges give us the
connections before the renderer curves them above the surface.

Gabriel is a related alternative. For each candidate edge, form a ball whose diameter joins
its endpoints. If another point in that category lies strictly inside the ball, reject the
edge. This produces a more selective proximity network. The application has a small-group
special case described in the notes. Neither construction uses correlations between time
series or evidence of interaction between the countries.

> Include the full 240 seconds of observation and response. The invitation is reflective, never a request for visitors to disclose personal pain.

Let us begin with the experience of looking at a globe. It makes the world seem available
to us all at once. We can see a boundary, travel across an ocean with our eyes, and name
a country without knowing the life of a single person there. That power is useful, and
it is also a limitation. A world that fits inside an image has already been simplified.

P.A.I.N. begins inside that tension. Its name means Personal And Interconnected with Nature.
The Personal-Planetary-Pain Map brings four kinds of information together: physical health,
emotional language, environmental change, and economic circumstances. It uses their meeting
as an invitation to think about bodies and the conditions that sustain them.

Before we examine the sources, choose a place to look at. It may be familiar or unfamiliar.
Notice whether a word reaches you first, or a colour, or a shape. You do not have to tell
us why you chose it. Observation is enough to participate.

> Looking pause: 40 seconds. Allow a visitor to point without explaining a personal connection.

The first layer is physical. The artwork turns health information into a marked surface.
It gives statistical estimates a texture that can look strained, wounded, or scarred.
Those are metaphors in the material language of the artwork. They are not photographs of
bodies, and they do not identify the location of an individual person's wound.

The source attribution names the Global Burden of Disease study at IHME, together with
WorldPop population information. The six conditions are headache disorders, migraine,
low back pain, neck pain, osteoarthritis, and rheumatoid arthritis. The map uses 15,282
spatial records, with 2,547 for each condition. This count describes the exported data
that enters the visualization, not a count of people who consented to tell a story here.

The project describes distributing country-based health information according to population.
That is one way to give a national estimate a location. It brings the possible concentration
of a burden into view, while also making an assumption about how information should be
placed. The drawing then adds overlapping depressions, smoothing, and a dotted surface.
The dots help us see the form. They should not be mistaken for a series of individual lives.

When we look at India, this distinction becomes especially important. Its high physical

average, and it cannot tell us how one person's pain compares with another's. A population-based

need evidence beyond that placement.

We might be tempted to turn a dramatic mark into a dramatic national story. The more
careful invitation is to ask what the mark has made visible and which lives remain
unavailable to this form of representation. A faint area does not tell us that life there
is easy. It may reflect the source, the coverage, the normalization, and the scale at
which we are looking.

> Reflection pause: 40 seconds. Ask what a texture can communicate that a table cannot, and what a table can preserve that a texture may hide.

The environmental layer asks us to look at the space around the surface. One input concerns
temperature change and is attributed to Berkeley Earth. Another concerns emissions and is
attributed to Climate TRACE. The artwork gives them an atmospheric presence. We are
looking at a visual translation of quantities, not a photograph of the air.

Temperature change has a particular relationship to memory. It compares a place with
an earlier condition rather than telling us only how it feels today. A cold region can
experience substantial warming. Russia and Canada have high warming peaks in the map's
country profiles. The point of that example is not to name a winner in a ranking. It is
to notice that a familiar image of a cold place may conceal an unfamiliar history of change.

The atmosphere's shape is part of the artwork's expression. Its height is not a measured
altitude. Its thickness is not a direct measure of exposure. The emissions export is
labelled CO2, but its exact original gas measure and time period still require confirmation.
That uncertainty does not need to disappear for us to look meaningfully. It needs to remain
visible in the explanation, so the image does not speak with more certainty than its source.

The phrase planetary pain is similarly an invitation to interpretation. We are not claiming
that the Earth has a nervous system like a person's. The metaphor lets us consider harm,
dependence, and care across scales. A forest, a body, an economy, and a community can all
be affected by change, but their experiences and mechanisms are not interchangeable.

There is something productive about bringing those differences close without erasing them.
An artwork can hold together questions that are separated in a database. We can explore
relations without pretending that every relation has already been measured, or that every
line in an image establishes a causal link in the world.

> Looking pause: 40 seconds. Let visitors compare surface and atmosphere without deciding that one explains the other.

The socioeconomic layer adds a yellow signal. Its selected source is World Bank GDP per
person for 2024. GDP measures economic output. A per-person figure divides that output by
population. The artwork uses a logarithmic transformation, then reverses the direction so
that lower output per person appears stronger in the pain layer.

This translation is deliberately open to questions. A measure of production is not a measure
of affection, security, fair distribution, or dignity. The same country figure can sit above
people with very different lives. The map cannot tell us the experience of the person whose

illuminates and what else an account of care would have to include.

should not be filled with a comforting story.

One word cannot contain the many forms of distress that may occur together. Grief and
anger can coexist. A word translated as hardship may carry associations that differ

and not to others. These are reasons to keep translation open to conversation, rather
than treating the lexicon as the final meaning of a culture.

story about uncertainty in Palau, we should recognize how small the sample is and how much

permission to speak on behalf of a country.

# How the economic scale works

For raw GDP per capita g, minimum a, and maximum b:

Y = 1 - (ln(g) - ln(a)) / (ln(b) - ln(a)).

Here a = 216.231928531758 (Burundi) and b = 288001.574856495 (Monaco).
The mapping gives Burundi Y = 1 and Monaco Y = 0. India's g = 2591.9916607122 gives
Y = 0.654754, rounded to six decimals. A numerical endpoint of zero is not a claim of
zero suffering. Missing GDP values do not enter this formula. [L4]

# Methods / From text and points to the globe

## The emotional pipeline

[[DIAGRAM:pipeline]]

PCAI acquires, prepares, and country-codes admitted text. For news it uses publisher-domain
reference information, a Public Suffix List, and usable country-code domain evidence.
GDELT contributes publisher reference metadata; it is not a hidden seventh text collection
in the selected emotional product. Expression records use their approved geographic context,
with an admission confidence floor of 0.70. Missing country context is not simply filled
from place names mentioned in prose. [L2]

pcai-classifier uses frozen BGE-M3 embeddings and a learned prediction head. The training-data
program assembled 573,143 synthetic examples across 100 languages. That is the available
package count, not the exact final-fit count for bundle-v9: its recipe excludes a legacy
bridge source. Training examples must remain distinct from the 249,438 real-text inference
inputs. The older small diagnostic model and the later full news run are different products.

The public group score composes a distress-scope score and a conditional group score:
P(group) = P(distress scope) x P(group given distress). Multiple groups can receive signal
from the same text. These model outputs need not sum to one and are not clinical scores.

For country c and category k, let N be the mean over all selected news rows and E the mean
over all selected expression rows pooled across their five source collections.

| Available cohorts | Country score | Countries |
| News and expressions | C = 0.75 N + 0.25 E | 169 |
| News only | C = N | 17 |
| Expressions only | C = E | 6 |
| Neither, within the 195-country source roster | Missing | 3 |

The denominator is all classified rows in the cohort, not only rows assigned a positive
distress label. Public group scores are averaged even for rows whose decoded scope is
uncertain, no-pain, or out-of-scope. The displayed table uses these raw weighted means,
rounded to four decimal places. A separate analytical within-country min-max heatmap must
not be substituted for it. All 3,072 observed values (192 x 16 columns) match the selected
weighted calculation at the stored precision. [L2]

## What the model validation does and does not establish

The saved selected predictions have accepted_model=false and real_world_validated=false.
They are diagnostic outputs. The training roster includes 100 languages, while the saved
release declaration lists English, Spanish, and Arabic. Wider processing is best-effort
diagnostic use, not verified equal accuracy across the language roster. Of the news probe,
2,605 rows have language hints in that declared set and 2,479 were truncated at the documented
512-token input limit. A token is a model's text unit, not always a complete word. [L2]

These facts do not supply a measured error rate, a national gold standard, or confidence
intervals for country ranks. A large sample cannot eliminate model bias, unrepresentative
sources, or incorrect geography. The current score comparisons should be narrated as
properties of this selected corpus and model.

## Spatial profiles and visual fields

For physical, temperature, and emissions data, the app assigns coordinate points to its
Natural Earth polygons and selects the maximum relevant value. It retains the contributing
point count. Physical pools all six condition categories for that maximum. Temperature and
emissions remain separate. A point count describes export coverage, not patient sampling.
Simplified boundaries can affect assignment near coastlines and small countries. [L3, L5]

The globe surface and the country symbols are separate renderings. Physical points stamp
overlapping depressions into a field that is smoothed. Temperature and emissions use their
own accumulation, smoothing, and appearance controls. A rendered haze cannot be read as a
linear ruler of the original source variable. Country area symbols use a square-root size
mapping so their filled area represents their normalized signal; the emissions band has
its own treatment. [L5]

Country locations for words and graph nodes use cartographic label points and some manually
specified small-country positions. They are not necessarily geometric centroids. The
geography/profile union has 206 entries, whereas the emotional input has 192 observed
countries. Keep those denominators separate. A missing symbol may reflect unavailable
data or geography; do not describe every blank location as an observed zero.

## The network, precisely enough to explain

[[DIAGRAM:graph]]

Each observed country's selected emotion assigns it to a category group. Its geographic
label position becomes a unit vector on the globe. For four or more nodes, the current
Delaunay rule builds a three-dimensional convex hull and uses the unique edges of its faces.
The renderer draws curved great-circle arcs above the surface. [L5]

Gabriel mode starts from those candidate edges. For endpoints a and b, take midpoint
m = (a + b) / 2 and squared radius r2 = distance(a,m)^2. An edge is rejected if another
point c in the same category satisfies distance(c,m)^2 < r2 - 1e-12. It is an empty
three-dimensional diameter-ball test, not a test on screen-space text positions.
Points on the boundary do not reject the edge. [L5]

Implementation qualification: for fewer than four nodes, the app returns all pairs even
in Gabriel mode. Near-coincident or near-antipodal endpoints can also be omitted by the arc
renderer. Do not quote historical graph-edge counts as current: category membership and
filters change the graph. The current selected graph is Delaunay, not Gabriel.

The reveal sequence uses graph steps outward from the selected origin. This is interaction
animation, not a temporal model. Lines do not encode correlation, influence, transport,
friendship, shared exposure, or transmission. Geographic proximity and category membership
are enough to construct them; no interpersonal or causal evidence is required.

## Practical exhibition operation

Begin with all layers and orient the globe before introducing numbers. Select the emotional
layer to expose its category controls, then a category to reveal its network. Repeat
selection can clear the network. Excluding a category reranks the remaining categories,
so explain the change in question before discussing the new pattern. Restore all categories
before quoting the winner counts in this guide. [L5]

The projection mode hides the visitor-survey action. The related web mode can offer a
survey and generated response, which is a separate participation flow and not a seventh
source in the displayed country dataset. Do not ask a projection audience to disclose
personal health information or describe an absent interface control as available.

## Unresolved methods to keep visible

- Physical: original GBD release, year, variable, age/sex strata, uncertainty, population
  raster year, allocation, and normalization.
- Temperature: original Berkeley Earth file, baseline calculation, exact temporal selection,
  scaling, and treatment of values outside the described reference range.
- Emissions: original Climate TRACE release, gas, units, time period, sectors, and scaling.
- Emotional: complete publication/crawl-date semantics, source representativeness, and
  validation of the intended country-level interpretation.

These are targeted information gaps. They do not prevent explaining the inspected
application, but they limit physical-unit conversions and causal country claims.

# Story collection / 25 ways to begin a conversation

Choose a few stories for a visit, not all 25. The Say paragraphs are original presenter wording.
Evidence and Keep in mind are background notes. These stories extend the nine complete scripts.
All map values refer to the selected exhibition data checked on 9 September 2026. Spatial
comparisons use the same 177 country/territory geometries as the application. Near-minimum
examples exclude missing observations; disputed territories are not presented as sovereign-country
rank winners. Numbers compare the named signal only. [L1-L5]

## Story 01 / India: one place, four different questions

**Say:** India can look intense on this globe, but there is no single number called India's pain.
Its physical symbol takes a local maximum from a migraine-labelled point. Its emotional word
comes from selected texts. Its warming and emissions symbols use other local maxima. Its
yellow signal comes from economic output per person. Seeing them together invites a question
about relationships. It does not prove that one caused another. The first useful explanation
for the physical concentration is how country-based health information was placed using
population data. Understanding medical causes would require the original health measure too.

**Evidence:** Physical peak 0.94266, 266 spatial records, with the peak in Migraine near
19.105 N, 72.913 E; temperature peak 0.151085 from 31 points; emissions peak 0.25547 from
40 points; GDP per capita US$2,591.99; emotional anger score 0.2331 from 27 news and 1,442
expression records. [L2-L4]

**Keep in mind:** A mapped concentration is not an individual risk estimate or a national mean.

## Story 02 / Pakistan: two headache labels, not two patient totals

**Say:** Pakistan has a high mapped peak in both Headache disorders and Migraine. That gives
us a chance to ask what a category means before adding numbers. Migraine and a broader
headache category should not automatically be treated as separate groups of people. Here the
country profile takes a maximum; it does not add the two condition surfaces. A value of one
means reaching an endpoint of the exported normalized surface. It does not mean that everybody
has the condition, or that we know Pakistan's national rate from this image.

**Evidence:** Both condition peaks are 1.00000, each based on 13 assigned condition points.
The combined physical profile receives 82 records. [L3]

**Keep in mind:** The exact upstream health hierarchy and normalization are unresolved.

## Story 03 / Japan: back pain and the question of age

**Say:** Japan's low-back-pain surface reaches a normalized peak of one. A tempting explanation
would immediately connect that result to age. WHO does identify age and population growth
as important to the global burden of low back pain. But that context does not establish why
this particular Japanese point is high. We first need the original dataset's age structure,
whether it reports counts or rates, and how population was used in mapping. The story teaches
us to turn a plausible explanation into a question that can be checked.

**Evidence:** Low back pain peak 1.00000 from 17 condition points; 102 records contribute to
Japan's all-condition physical maximum. WHO context is general, not an analysis of this map. [L3, C3]

**Keep in mind:** Do not present ageing as a demonstrated cause of Japan's displayed rank.

## Story 04 / China: arthritis is not one condition

**Say:** China's high physical values include both osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.
The shared word arthritis can hide differences. Osteoarthritis affects the joint and is
associated with factors including age and injury. Rheumatoid arthritis involves immune-driven
inflammation. Their routes into a health dataset are not interchangeable. The globe's country
symbol compresses the condition surfaces into one maximum. Opening the story back up means
asking which condition produced a value and what evidence would explain that condition.

**Evidence:** Both mapped condition peaks reach 1.00000; osteoarthritis has 135 assigned points
and rheumatoid arthritis 137. The combined physical profile has 803 records. [L3, C5-C6]

**Keep in mind:** These are spatial export counts, not patient totals or disease prevalence.

## Story 05 / Egypt: a strong neck-pain point

**Say:** Egypt is a useful example of how a very high-looking score can tempt us into a story
we have not verified. Its neck-pain surface reaches almost one. That observation alone does
not identify posture, work, treatment access, or any other cause. The country's shape on the

**Keep in mind:** Possible occupational or lifestyle explanations have not been tested here.

# Questions visitors may ask

## Does the globe show which country suffers most?

No single ranking is established. A meaningful comparison names a layer, its source variable,
time period, sample or point count, and aggregation. The layers do not share a validated unit
of suffering. Some source transformations remain unverified.

## Why are so many words anger?

With all categories enabled, anger is the highest retained score in 152 of 192 observed
countries. That is a property of this selected corpus, weighting, model, and winner rule.
It does not mean most residents are angry. Source composition and classifier behavior may
contribute, but their causal contributions have not been measured here.

## Are the lines Gabriel graphs?

The selected exhibition uses Delaunay graphs. Gabriel is a related option implemented during
development. Its empty-diameter-ball test removes some Delaunay candidate edges in the usual
four-or-more-node case. Both are geographic constructions inside category groups.

## Why does a word change when a category is excluded?

The display chooses the highest remaining category. Scores have not changed; the set of
eligible answers has. Category membership and therefore the graph can change too.

## Is this live data?

It is a saved selected product. The text sources span uneven historical years, GDP refers
to 2024, and precise health/environmental source releases remain partly unresolved.
The date of opening the website or exporting this guide is not the observation date.

## Does GDP per person mean personal wealth?

It is economic production divided by population. It does not measure wealth holdings,
a typical wage, income distribution, or purchasing-power-adjusted living standards.

## Can I infer a disease or emotional condition about somebody from this?

The country layers do not assess individual visitors or identify patients. An individual's
health and experience require a different kind of evidence and conversation.

# Glossary / Words worth keeping close

| Term | Plain explanation |
| Aggregation | Combining many observations into a summary, such as a mean or maximum. |
| Baseline | A reference condition used to calculate a change. |
| Classifier | A model that assigns category scores to an input. |
| Corpus | A collection of texts selected for analysis. |
| Convex hull | The smallest convex shape enclosing a set of points, like a tight shell around them. |
| Denominator | The quantity being divided by; it changes what a rate or average means. |
| Diagnostic output | An exploratory result whose intended real-world interpretation is not validated. |
| Delaunay | A proximity construction; this app uses edges of the convex hull of spherical points. |
| Gabriel | A proximity rule using an empty ball whose diameter joins two candidate endpoints. |
| Inference | Applying a trained model to new input. |
| Language hint | Metadata about likely language, not proof of model accuracy. |
| Logarithm | A transformation that makes multiplicative differences easier to compare. |
| Maximum | The largest observed value, not the average. |
| Missing | An observation is unavailable; it is not automatically zero. |
| Normalization | A numerical rescaling; the formula and reference range must be specified. |
| Per capita | Per person, using the relevant population denominator. |
| Prevalence | The proportion or number of people with a condition in a defined population and period. |
| Proxy | A variable used to stand in for a broader concept it does not measure completely. |
| Sampling bias | A selected collection differs systematically from the population of interest. |
| Solastalgia | Distress associated with unwanted change to a familiar home environment. |
| Spatial allocation | Placing aggregate information across geographic locations using a rule or model. |
| Synthetic training data | Constructed examples used for learning, distinct from observed input records. |
| Token | A model's unit of text, which may be a word part or punctuation. |
| Uncertainty | What remains unresolved about a value or interpretation, beyond its printed precision. |

## Keeping this edition useful

Before reusing a numerical story after a dataset change, check its metric, source, denominator,
and date against the new selection. A later classifier run or a changed category filter can
produce different labels. Preserve the distinction between a change in evidence and a change
in visual treatment. Additional source-method material can improve the explicitly open sections
without rewriting supported observations into stronger claims.
